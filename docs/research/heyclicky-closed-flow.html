Your second question — traced end to end

What happens when you hold the key and circle something

Click each step. EVIDENCE lines are strings or symbols found verbatim in the binary.

1
HoldYou press and hold ctrl + option
A global CGEvent tap — installed system-wide, watching modifier and mouse events — recognizes the chord. A chime plays, and a realtime voice session opens (or a warm one is reused). Nothing was watching before this: capture is strictly gated on the hotkey. EVIDENCE · "Global push-to-talk: couldn't create modifier/mouse event tap" · "[Realtime] PTT begin: reusing healthy session" · "I only see your screen when you press the hotkey." · marketing copy in-binary: "hold ctrl+option and talk about anything on screen"
2
FollowA companion appears at your pointer — and follows it
The OverlayWindowManager raises a transparent, click-through OverlayWindow on every connected display. Riding on it is BlueCursorView — the blue thing at your cursor — with a live waveform while you speak, a spinner while it thinks, a stop icon. It tracks you via a mouse-location polling timer + NSTrackingArea. So "it knows where my trackpad is" = the app polls the global pointer position the whole time the chord is held. It is not streaming video anywhere at this point — it's just following the pointer. EVIDENCE · symbols: OverlayWindowManager.overlayWindowsByScreenID, BlueCursorWaveformView, BlueCursorSpinnerView, mouseLocationPollingTimer, _lastObservedMouseLocation, NSTrackingArea
3
SpeakYour voice streams live over two socket rails
Audio goes to OpenAI's Realtime API (wss://api.openai.com/v1/realtime?model=gpt-realtime-2.1) for the conversational assistant, and Deepgram (wss://api.deepgram.com/v1/listen) powers the dictation rail. There's real acoustic-echo-cancellation plumbing so it can hear you over its own speech. EVIDENCE · both wss URLs verbatim · "gpt-realtime-2.1" · "[Realtime] AEC topology verdict stored" · DictationDeepgramTranscriber.swift
4
DrawMoving the pointer while holding = ink on the overlay
The overlay window doubles as a canvas. ScreenAnnotationManager records your strokes and classifies their shape — an enclosing loop ("circled the …") vs a line ("drew a line across the …"). It also records dwell events: pause the pointer somewhere (there's a dwell radius + minimum duration) and that hover itself becomes part of the gesture — you can point without drawing. All of this is compressed into a "PTT gesture summary." EVIDENCE · ScreenAnnotationManager.Stroke/.StrokeShape/.DwellEvent · dwellRadius, dwellMinimumDuration, dwell_ms · "[Annotation] PTT gesture summary:" · "circled the", "scribbled over the", "drew a line from the", "had no enclosing shape"
5
ReleaseKey up → NOW the screen is captured
This answers "how is it capturing the screen at that point": it mostly isn't, until you let go. On key-up the audio commits (with a ~400 ms tail) and a ScreenCaptureKit capture fires — a still capture of the screen, persisted with your drawn highlight baked into the image. Multi-display is handled (the coordinate grammar carries a screenN suffix). So: not a continuous recording — a per-turn photograph, taken at the moment your question ends. EVIDENCE · SCShareableContent, SCStreamConfiguration · "[Realtime] PTT end tail cancelled after screen capture" · "PTT end: scheduling 400ms tail, then commit" · "failed to persist annotated capture" · "screenshot images show their screen WITH the drawn highlight" · regex fragment (?::screen(\d+))?
6
HarvestThe circled region gets a second, structural read
Here's the trick that makes region answers precise: an AnnotationAccessibilityHarvester walks the accessibility tree inside your circle and extracts the real element strings and URLs. The model is told to trust those over its own OCR: "Accessibility data inside the highlighted region (use these exact strings/URLs when relevant, don't re-read them from the image)." Pixels for context, AX for ground truth. EVIDENCE · AnnotationAccessibilityHarvester.HarvestedElement · "[Annotation] AX context:" · the quoted prompt string verbatim
7
AnswerThe model is ordered to focus on what you marked
The turn ships: annotated screenshot(s) + gesture summary + harvested AX elements + your transcript. The prompt is blunt: "look at the highlighted region and answer their actual question about it, brief and specific. Do not deflect and do not say you can't see the screen" — and "focus on what they marked." That's why the response locks onto your circle and nothing else. Harder questions escalate over /chat-tool-call to a "higher model" with explicit annotation_elements and annotation_gesture payload fields. EVIDENCE · both prompt strings verbatim · "higher model annotation_elements:" · "[Realtime] /chat-tool-call raw response"
8
Draw back…and it can annotate your screen in return
The response isn't only speech. The model emits tag-first "beat" lines with pixel coordinates — a little drawing language (next section) that Swift parses with regexes and renders on the same overlay: rings around targets, arrows for motion, highlights for work areas. Walkthroughs loop: after you act, it re-captures, verifies your click landed, and moves to the next beat — with rules against re-pointing at the same button twice. EVIDENCE · parse regexes for [POINT:…] / [SHAPE:…] · "run screenshot walkthrough beats=… captures=…" · "Do not repeat completed targets unless the new screenshot proves the action failed"

The proprietary grammar

The beat language — how a model draws on your Mac

Reverse-engineered from the parser regexes and prompt instructions in the binary. Each "beat" is one tag-first line; Swift renders the tag, and the sentence after it is spoken/shown.

[TARGET:x,y,r:label]

A click target — ring at (x,y) radius r. Only for the ONE next action the app can observe, re-capture, and verify. "The TARGET sentence must contain only that one immediate action."

[HOVER:x,y,r:label]

Same, for hover-reveals the app can detect ("Hovered guided target").

[POINT:x,y:label] · [POINT:none]

Point at an exact handle/control without demanding a click. none = explicitly no visual this turn. Optional :screenN suffix for multi-display.

[HIGHLIGHT]

Marks the general work area for manual tasks (typing, dragging a knob, picking a color) — used when faking a click target would be dishonest.

[SHAPE:circle:cx,cy;ex,ey:label]

An enclosing ring: center + a point on the edge "just past the thing." The same vocabulary you use when you circle — now spoken back at you.

[SHAPE:line|arrow|curve|polygon:…]

Motion and region shapes — arrows for "drag from here to here," polygons for irregular areas.

// prompt excerpt, verbatim from the binary:
"Use exactly one tag-first [TARGET:x,y,r:label] or [HOVER:x,y,r:label] only when the
 next visible step is a click/tap/focus/commit or hover reveal that Swift can observe,
 recapture, and continue from. Example: [TARGET:120,210,40:add modifier] Click Add
 Modifier to open the list. … If the next step is manual work Swift cannot reliably
 detect as complete … do NOT fake a target. Use [HIGHLIGHT] for the work area, [POINT]
 for the exact handle/control, and [SHAPE:arrow] or [SHAPE:curve] for the motion."
SHARP

Notice the honesty rule baked into the grammar: a TARGET is only allowed when the app can verify completion from a fresh capture. Anything it can't verify gets downgraded to HIGHLIGHT/POINT + words. Capability truthfulness enforced at the protocol level — same philosophy as hiding the recording tools in Part 1.

"Is it recording the screen?"

The capture model, precisely

MomentWhat's capturedWhere it goes

While holdingPointer position (local overlay tracking) + mic audioAudio → OpenAI Realtime / Deepgram sockets
On releaseOne ScreenCaptureKit still, annotated with your inkModel turn (+ persisted locally for the worker)

SO

Event-gated stills, not a video stream. The "recording" feeling comes from three overlays composited live and locally (cursor companion, your ink, its beats) — while actual pixel egress is a photograph per conversational turn. That's a much easier privacy story than continuous capture, and it's the design worth copying.

What I think about it

Read on the strategy — and on us

The open/closed split is the tell. They open-sourced the layer that was already a commodity (background clicking via AX) and kept every gram of the experience: the hotkey gate, the cursor companion, the ink pipeline, the AX harvest, the beat grammar, the voice stack. The moat isn't "can an AI click a button" — it's that voice + pointer + ink arrive at the model as one fused gesture. Nobody else's turn contains "the user said X while circling Y and pausing over Z."

The engineering is disciplined, not exotic. Every piece is a standard macOS primitive — CGEvent tap, transparent NSWindows, ScreenCaptureKit stills, an AX walk — composed with unusual care (dwell detection, echo cancellation, verify-before-next-beat, don't-repeat-targets). Which means the flow is fully replicable for Cortex; what takes taste is the grammar and the restraint.

Two dependencies worth noting: the assistant personality currently rides on OpenAI's Realtime API (gpt-realtime-2.1) with Deepgram as the second rail — a real vendor coupling — and heavier turns route through their worker at api.heyclicky.com, so the "local" app has a cloud spine.

adoptThe fused turn

Cortex's screen plane should treat {transcript + gesture summary + annotated still + region AX harvest} as ONE input event. This is the single highest-leverage idea in the teardown.

adoptEvent-gated capture

Never stream. Capture on turn boundaries, gate on an explicit hold, and be able to say the sentence "it only sees when you press."

adoptAX inside the ink

The circle isn't just a crop hint — harvest the accessibility elements under it and hand the model ground-truth strings. Pixels orient, structure answers.

designA beat-like output grammar

Define our own tag-first visual protocol early — with the same honesty rule (only promise a target you can verify). It doubles as the UI contract between model and native host.
